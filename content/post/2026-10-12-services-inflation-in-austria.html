Services inflation in Austria has been stubborn. Energy and food prices have long stopped rising at the pace of 2022, but services, which make up half of the Austrian consumer basket, are still getting more expensive by more than 4% a year. Hochmuth and Petz (2026) offer an explanation for the euro area: before the pandemic, services became steadily more expensive relative to goods, the surge of goods prices in 2021 and 2022 pushed this relative price far below its trend, and part of today’s services inflation is services prices catching up. Their error correction model says how fast that happens.

This post applies their model to Austria. The reproduction of the paper covers the euro area in detail, and its results serve as the benchmark here. The short answer is that the explanation does not carry over: in Austria the relative price of services has already returned to its trend and gone beyond it. A closer look shows that most of this is due to one item that weighs much more in Austria than elsewhere: restaurants and cafés.

The idea in one equation

Let \(s_t\) and \(n_t\) be 100 times the log price indices of services and of non-energy industrial goods (NEIG), the goods part of core inflation. The relative price of services \(s_t - n_t\) is assumed to follow a linear trend, from which it deviates only temporarily:

\[s_t - n_t = \mu + \delta t + z_t.\]

The deviation \(z_t\) is the relative-price gap. It is estimated as the residual of an OLS regression on a trend, and enters a Bayesian VECM as error correction term:

\[\Delta y_t = \kappa + \alpha \hat{z}_{t-1} + \Gamma \Delta y_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t, \quad \Delta y_t = (\Delta s_t, \Delta n_t)'.\]

The loadings \(\alpha = (\alpha_s, \alpha_n)'\) are the interesting part. A negative \(\alpha_s\) means that services inflation picks up when services are cheap relative to their trend, a positive \(\alpha_n\) that goods inflation slows. Both close the gap. The priors are those of the paper: standard normal on \(\kappa\), \(\alpha\) and \(\Gamma\), and \(\Sigma \sim IW(4, 0.01 I)\).

What drives Austrian inflation?

Eurostat publishes the contributions of the main components to annual inflation only for the euro area, so they have to be calculated for Austria. The HICP is a chain-linked index: within a year, it is a weighted average of its components relative to their level in the previous December, with that year’s weights. The annual rate in a month therefore spans two links, from the same month a year earlier to December and from December to the current month, which carry different weights. Splitting both links into the components and adding them up gives the contribution of component \(i\) in month \(t\) of year \(y\) (Ribe, 1999):

\[c_{i,t} = \frac{100}{P_{t-12}} \left[ w_{i,y} P_{D(y-1)} \left( \frac{P^i_t}{P^i_{D(y-1)}} - 1 \right) + w_{i,y-1} P_{D(y-2)} \frac{P^i_{D(y-1)} - P^i_{t-12}}{P^i_{D(y-2)}} \right],\]

where \(P\) is the overall index, \(P^i\) the index of component \(i\), \(w_{i,y}\) its weight in year \(y\) as a share of the total and \(D(y)\) December of year \(y\). The four components – energy, food including alcohol and tobacco, NEIG and services – make up the whole basket, so their contributions add up to headline inflation. Applied to the euro area indices, the formula matches the contributions that Eurostat publishes to within 0.01 percentage points.

The formula is needed again further below, so it goes into a function. It works on the months of one component in order, where weight is the share of the component in the aggregate in the year of each month:

contribution <- function(month, index, total, weight) {
  year <- as.integer(substr(month, 1, 4))
  december_1 <- match(paste0(year - 1, "-12"), month)
  december_2 <- match(paste0(year - 2, "-12"), month)

  100 / lag(total, 12) *
    (weight * total[december_1] * (index / index[december_1] - 1) +
       lag(weight, 12) * total[december_2] *
         (index[december_1] - lag(index, 12)) / index[december_2])
}

Services fell behind goods, and caught up

Chart 2 of the brief shows the price of services relative to goods as a four-quarter moving average with a log-linear trend fitted over 1996 to 2019 and extrapolated from there. The same chart for Austria, with the euro area series of the reproduction for comparison, uses quarterly averages of the unadjusted indices. The following function turns the monthly indices into quarterly averages, unadjusted for the chart and seasonally adjusted for the model below:

prepare_prices <- function(hicp) {

  monthly <- hicp %>%
    select(month, SERV, IGD_NNRG, GD) %>%
    pivot_longer(-month, names_to = "series", values_to = "index") %>%
    arrange(series, month) %>%

    # X-13ARIMA-SEATS with the defaults of seasonal, series by series
    group_by(series) %>%
    mutate(index_sa = ts(index, start = c(1996, 1), frequency = 12) %>%
             seas() %>%
             final() %>%
             as.numeric()) %>%
    ungroup()

  # Quarterly averages, dated by the start of the quarter: 1996.25 is 1996 Q2
  quarterly <- monthly %>%
    mutate(year = as.integer(substr(month, 1, 4)),
           month_of_year = as.integer(substr(month, 6, 7)),
           quarter = year + (month_of_year - 1) %/% 3 / 4) %>%
    group_by(series, quarter) %>%
    summarise(index = mean(index),
              index_sa = mean(index_sa),
              .groups = "drop")

  return(quarterly)
}

Before the pandemic, services in Austria became 1.5% a year more expensive relative to NEIG, against 1.3% in the euro area, and 0.7% relative to overall goods, against 0.3%. Up to 2021 the Austrian relative prices followed their trends closely, and in 2022 and 2023 they fell below them much like those of the euro area. The difference is what happened next. In the euro area the relative price of services is still 3.2% below its extrapolated trend against NEIG and 5% below it against overall goods in the second quarter of 2026. In Austria, services prices rose so much faster than goods prices from 2023 onwards that the relative price crossed its trend in 2025, and it is now 2.5% above it against NEIG and 1.3% above it against overall goods.

Estimation

Even though the catch-up seems to be over, the model is worth estimating, because it says how strongly Austrian prices respond to a gap at all. The estimation follows the reproduction. A function runs both stages for a country and either goods index: an OLS regression of the relative price on a trend over the whole sample, whose lagged residual enters create_bvarmodel as exogenous variable without further lags, s = 0. add_priors sets the paper’s priors, and the sampler is run for 5,000 burn-in draws, after which 20,000 are kept. The euro area is estimated again alongside Austria with the same code.

estimate_gap_model <- function(prices, other) {

  # Log prices of services (s) and of the other index (n), times 100
  y <- prices %>%
    filter(series %in% c("SERV", other)) %>%
    transmute(quarter,
              series = if_else(series == "SERV", "s", "n"),
              value = 100 * log(index_sa)) %>%
    pivot_wider(names_from = series, values_from = value) %>%
    arrange(quarter) %>%
    mutate(trend = row_number())

  # Stage 1: the relative-price gap is the residual of a linear trend
  stage_1 <- lm(s - n ~ trend, data = y)
  gap <- residuals(stage_1)

  # Stage 2: a VAR(1) in differences, with the lagged gap as exogenous
  # regressor
  dy <- ts(diff(as.matrix(y[, c("s", "n")])),
           start = y[["quarter"]][2],
           frequency = 4)

  ect <- ts(matrix(head(gap, -1), dimnames = list(NULL, "ect")),
            start = y[["quarter"]][2],
            frequency = 4)

  model <- create_bvarmodel(dy,
                            p = 1,
                            exogen = ect,
                            s = 0,
                            deterministic = "const",
                            iterations = 20000,
                            burnin = 5000)

  model <- add_priors(model,
                      coef = list(v_i = 1, v_i_det = 1),
                      sigma = list(df = 4, scale = 0.01))

  model <- add_initial_values(model, method = "ols")

  return(add_posterior_coefficients(model))
}

How fast does the gap close?

The paper simulates the model in deviations from a baseline without a gap. From an initial gap \(z_0 = -1\), services about 1% cheaper relative to goods than their trend implies, and \(\Delta y_0 = 0\), the system evolves as

\[\Delta y_h = \alpha z_{h-1} + \Gamma \Delta y_{h-1}, \quad z_h = z_{h-1} + \Delta s_h - \Delta n_h,\]

and the cumulative contributions of services and goods inflation to closing it, in percent of the initial gap, are \(C_{s,h} = 100 \sum_{j \le h} \Delta s_j\) and \(C_{n,h} = -100 \sum_{j \le h} \Delta n_j\). The recursion is run for every posterior draw at once:

gap_closure <- function(model, horizon = 40) {

  # Equation i's coefficient on regressor j is in column (j - 1) k + i. The
  # regressors are the lags of s and n, the error correction term and the
  # constant
  draws <- as.matrix(model[["posterior"]][["a"]][["coeffs"]])
  k <- model[["model"]][["k"]]

  gamma_ss <- draws[, 1]
  gamma_ns <- draws[, 2]
  gamma_sn <- draws[, k + 1]
  gamma_nn <- draws[, k + 2]
  alpha_s <- draws[, 2 * k + 1]
  alpha_n <- draws[, 2 * k + 2]

  # Start from z_0 = -1 and dy_0 = 0
  n_draws <- nrow(draws)
  z <- rep(-1, n_draws)
  ds <- rep(0, n_draws)
  dn <- rep(0, n_draws)
  services <- rep(0, n_draws)
  other <- rep(0, n_draws)

  paths <- vector("list", horizon)

  for (h in seq_len(horizon)) {

    ds_new <- alpha_s * z + gamma_ss * ds + gamma_sn * dn
    dn_new <- alpha_n * z + gamma_ns * ds + gamma_nn * dn

    ds <- ds_new
    dn <- dn_new
    z <- z + ds - dn

    # Cumulative contributions, in % of the initial gap
    services <- services + 100 * ds
    other <- other - 100 * dn

    paths[[h]] <- data.frame(h = h,
                             remaining = -100 * z,
                             services = services,
                             other = other)
  }

  closure <- bind_rows(paths) %>%
    group_by(h) %>%
    summarise(lower = quantile(remaining, 0.16),
              upper = quantile(remaining, 0.84),
              remaining = median(remaining),
              services = median(services),
              other = median(other))

  start <- data.frame(h = 0,
                      lower = 100,
                      upper = 100,
                      remaining = 100,
                      services = 0,
                      other = 0)

  return(bind_rows(start, closure))
}

Relative to NEIG, the gap closes about as fast in Austria as in the euro area: half of it is gone after 14 quarters, against 15. In both, services inflation does most of the work, but in different ways. In the euro area it responds strongly and has to make up for NEIG inflation, which works against the adjustment. In Austria the response of services inflation is weak but persistent – the coefficient of its own lag is above 0.7 – so it builds up over many quarters, and NEIG inflation does not work against it. A closer look at gastronomy

Austria is a tourism country, and it shows in its consumer basket. The HICP measures the spending of all households on the domestic territory, including that of foreign tourists, so restaurants, cafés and hotels weigh much more than in most other member states. Eurostat publishes indices and weights for food and beverage serving services, here called gastronomy, and accommodation services from 1996, and weights for services as a whole from 2000:

Services excluding gastronomy

Does gastronomy also explain why the relative price of services in Austria is above its trend? To find out, the price of services is split into gastronomy and the rest. Eurostat does not publish an index of services excluding gastronomy, but it can be derived from the chain-linking: within a year, the services index relative to December is the weighted average of its components relative to December, so the same holds for services without gastronomy with weights rescaled to add up to one,

\[\frac{P^{x}_t}{P^{x}_{D(y-1)}} = \frac{1}{1 - s_y} \left( \frac{P^s_t}{P^s_{D(y-1)}} - s_y \frac{P^g_t}{P^g_{D(y-1)}} \right),\]

where \(s_y\) is the share of gastronomy in services in year \(y\). Chaining these links from December 2000, the first December for which the weights of both are available, gives the index:

chain_excluding <- function(month, total, index, share) {
  year <- as.integer(substr(month, 1, 4))
  december <- match(paste0(year - 1, "-12"), month)
  link <- (total / total[december] - share * index / index[december]) /
    (1 - share)

  # The first month is a December, from which the links are chained
  result <- rep(100, length(month))
  for (t in seq_along(month)[-1]) {
    result[t] <- result[december[t]] * link[t]
  }

  return(result)
}

gastronomy <- gastronomy %>%
  mutate(year = as.integer(substr(month, 1, 4))) %>%
  left_join(select(shares, geo, year, share = CP111), by = c("geo", "year")) %>%
  arrange(geo, month) %>%
  group_by(geo) %>%
  mutate(SERV_X_CP111 = chain_excluding(month, SERV, CP111, share)) %>%
  ungroup()

Gastronomy has been getting more expensive relative to NEIG for a long time, by 1.9% a year in Austria before the pandemic, against 1.6% in the euro area. Since 2022 the Austrian relative price has moved far away from that trend, and in the second quarter of 2026 it is 14.6% above it, while that of the euro area is within 1.7% of its trend. Without gastronomy, Austrian services are only 1% above their trend relative to NEIG, against 4% with it, and those of the euro area 3.1% below theirs. The figure for all services differs from the one in the first chart, because the trend is fitted from 2001 rather than 1996.

Gastronomy therefore explains most of the overshoot of the Austrian relative price. It does not explain why services inflation is still high, though: without gastronomy, Austrian services prices are 4.6% higher than a year earlier, almost as much as with it, and well above the 3.1% of the euro area.

What it means

Hochmuth and Petz argue that services inflation in the euro area stays high partly because the price of services has to catch up with that of goods after the shock of 2021 and 2022. For Austria, the data tell a different story:

  • The catch-up is over. The relative price of services fell below its trend in Austria about as much as in the euro area, but it has since climbed back above it, against NEIG and against overall goods. Whatever keeps Austrian services inflation above 4% today, it is not a gap that still has to be closed.
  • Gastronomy is behind the overshoot, but not behind today’s inflation. Restaurants and cafés weigh much more in Austria than in the euro area, their prices have risen by half since 2019 and they are far above their long-run trend relative to goods. Without them, the relative price of Austrian services is close to its trend. Today’s services inflation, however, is broad: services excluding gastronomy rise almost as fast.
  • If anything, the gap now argues for lower services inflation. The model is symmetric: with services above their trend relative to goods, it expects services inflation to slow and goods inflation to pick up. The direct response of Austrian services prices is weak and uncertain, though, so this pull is slow, and relative to overall goods it was goods prices that did the adjusting in the past.
  • Other explanations are needed. Candidates specific to Austria are wage settlements that take the inflation of the past year as their starting point, which pass the energy shock on to labour-intensive services such as gastronomy with a delay, strong demand from tourism, and the indexation of rents and many administered prices to past inflation. Testing them is beyond the scope of a model with two prices.

There are also reasons for caution. A straight line fitted to thirty years of data is sensitive to its end points, and the finding that Austria is above its trend depends on the trend not having steepened. And the gap is the residual of a first stage whose uncertainty is not carried over to the second, a simplification of the paper that this post keeps.
