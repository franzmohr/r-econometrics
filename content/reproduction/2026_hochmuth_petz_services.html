Euro area services inflation has come down much more slowly than goods inflation since its peak in 2023. Hochmuth and Petz (2026) read this partly as a relative-price adjustment. Before the pandemic, services prices rose faster than goods prices along a stable trend, which reflects slower productivity growth in services, income-elastic demand for them and their lower tradability. The surge in goods prices in 2021 and 2022 pushed the price of services relative to goods below that trend, and part of what looks like persistent services inflation is the relative price working its way back.

How fast that happens, and whether it happens through higher services inflation or lower goods inflation, is the question of the policy brief’s Annex A. It answers it with a small error correction model of services and non-energy industrial goods (NEIG) prices, and finds that half of a gap closes after about three and a half years, mostly through services. With overall goods prices instead of NEIG the gap closes within about five quarters, and mostly through goods.

This post reproduces both results with bvartools, together with the brief’s three descriptive charts: the composition of euro area inflation, and the relative price of services in the euro area and the United States.

The model

Let \(s_t = 100 \ln P^s_t\) and \(n_t = 100 \ln P^n_t\) be the log price indices of services and NEIG. The paper fixes the cointegrating vector at \(\beta = (1, -1)'\), so that \(\beta' y_t = s_t - n_t\) is the log relative price of services, and assumes that it follows a linear trend:

\[s_t - n_t = \mu + \delta t + z_t,\]

where \(z_t\) is stationary. The trend allows the two price indices to grow at different rates in the long run, and \(z_t\) is the deviation from it, the relative-price gap.

The model is estimated in two stages. The first is an OLS regression of the relative price on a constant and a trend, whose residual \(\hat{z}_t\) is the estimated gap. The second is a VECM that uses \(\hat{z}_{t-1}\) as its error correction term,

\[\Delta y_t = \kappa + \alpha \hat{z}_{t-1} + \Gamma \Delta y_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t, \quad \varepsilon_t \sim N(0, \Sigma),\]

with \(\Delta y_t = (\Delta s_t, \Delta n_t)'\). The loadings \(\alpha = (\alpha_s, \alpha_n)'\) say how services and NEIG inflation respond to a gap. The paper puts independent standard normal priors on \(\kappa\), \(\alpha\) and \(\Gamma\), and an inverse Wishart prior on \(\Sigma \sim IW(4, 0.01 I)\).

Since \(\hat{z}_{t-1}\) is data once the first stage is done, the second stage is a VAR(1) in \(\Delta y_t\) with one exogenous regressor, which is how it is estimated below.

The data

The paper uses monthly harmonised indices of consumer prices (HICP) for the euro area (changing composition) from Eurostat data set prc_hicp_minr, from January 1996 to June 2026. They are seasonally adjusted with X-13ARIMA-SEATS from the seasonal package and averaged to quarters.

The data set is the first that Eurostat publishes under the new ECOICOP version 2, and the vintage of October 2026 has no euro area index before December 1999, although the paper says it started in January 1996 when it was downloaded in August. The ECB Data Portal still has the old ECOICOP version 1 indices, which start in 1996, so the missing months are filled in with their month-on-month growth rates. The ECB’s NEIG index itself has a hole from January 1997 to November 2000, which its published annual rates of change fill: \(P_t = P_{t-12} (1 + \pi^{yoy}_t / 100)\). Since those rates have only one decimal, each year rebuilt that way is off by at most 0.05%.

The price of services relative to goods

Chart 2 of the brief shows the price of services relative to NEIG and to overall goods as a four-quarter moving average, indexed to 100 in the first quarter of 2000, together with a log-linear trend fitted over 1996 to 2019 and extrapolated from there. The moving average removes the seasonal pattern, so the chart works with quarterly averages of the unadjusted indices:

ma4 <- function(x) stats::filter(x, rep(1 / 4, 4), sides = 1)
index_2000 <- function(x) 100 * x / window(x, start = c(2000, 1), end = c(2000, 1))[1]

# A smoothed relative price and its log-linear trend, fitted up to 'fit_end'
relative_price <- function(services, goods, fit_end = Inf) {
  x <- index_2000(ma4(services / goods))
  t <- time(x)
  fit <- lm(log(x) ~ t, subset = t < fit_end)
  trend <- ts(exp(predict(fit, data.frame(t = t))), start = start(x), frequency = frequency(x))
  list(price = x, trend = trend, growth = 100 * coef(fit)[["t"]])
}

Before the pandemic services became 1.3% a year more expensive relative to NEIG, and 0.3% relative to overall goods. Both relative prices fell below their trends in 2021 and 2022. The one against overall goods fell much more, because the energy and food prices it includes rose most, and it has made up less of the loss since. In the second quarter of 2026 services relative to NEIG are 3.2% below the extrapolated trend, which the brief puts at 3.5%, and relative to overall goods 5% below it.

Estimation

A function sets up both stages for either price index, so that the benchmark and the specification with overall goods prices share the code. The first stage is lm. In the second, create_bvarmodel takes \(\hat{z}_{t-1}\) as an exogenous variable exogen with no further lags, s = 0, and add_priors sets the paper’s priors directly: a precision v_i = 1 is a standard normal prior with mean zero, and sigma = list(df = 4, scale = 0.01) is the inverse Wishart with four degrees of freedom and scale matrix \(0.01 I\). The sampler is run for 5,000 burn-in draws, and the next 20,000 are kept.

library("bvartools")

estimate_gap_model <- function(prices, other) {
  y <- 100 * log(prices[, c("services", other)])
  colnames(y) <- c("s", "n")

  # Stage 1: the relative-price gap
  rel <- y[, "s"] - y[, "n"]
  trend <- seq_along(rel)
  z <- ts(residuals(lm(rel ~ trend)), start = start(rel), frequency = 4)

  # Stage 2: a VAR(1) in differences with the lagged gap as exogenous regressor
  dy <- diff(y)
  ect <- window(stats::lag(z, -1), start = start(dy), end = end(dy))
  ect <- ts(matrix(ect, dimnames = list(NULL, "ect")), start = start(dy), frequency = 4)

  model <- create_bvarmodel(dy, p = 1, exogen = ect, s = 0, deterministic = "const",
                            iterations = 20000, burnin = 5000)
  model <- add_priors(model,
                      coef = list(v_i = 1, v_i_det = 1),
                      sigma = list(df = 4, scale = 0.01))
  model <- add_initial_values(model, method = "ols")
  add_posterior_coefficients(model)
}

A gap below trend, \(\hat{z}_{t-1} < 0\), raises services inflation: \(\alpha_s\) is negative and its credible interval excludes zero. The loading of NEIG inflation is negative as well, but smaller and not clearly different from zero. Since a gap closes when services inflation exceeds NEIG inflation, the NEIG response works against the adjustment.

How fast does the gap close?

To isolate the adjustment the paper simulates the model in deviations from a baseline without a gap. The intercepts and the errors then drop out, and from \(z_0 = -1\) and \(\Delta y_0 = 0\) the system evolves as

\[\Delta y_h = \alpha z_{h-1} + \Gamma \Delta y_{h-1}, \quad z_h = z_{h-1} + \Delta s_h - \Delta n_h, \quad h = 1, \ldots, H.\]

An initial gap of \(-1\) means that services are about 1% cheaper relative to NEIG than their trend implies. The cumulative contributions of services and NEIG inflation to closing it, in percent of the initial gap, are

\[C_{s,h} = 100 \sum_{j=1}^{h} \Delta s_j, \quad C_{n,h} = -100 \sum_{j=1}^{h} \Delta n_j,\]

and \(100 + 100 z_h = 100 - C_{s,h} - C_{n,h}\) is what remains of the gap after \(h\) quarters.

The draws of the coefficients are in posterior$a$coeffs. Each row is one draw of \(vec(A)\), where \(A\) has one row per equation and one column per regressor, in the order summary prints them. The recursion is run for all draws at once:

gap_closure <- function(model, horizon = 40) {
  draws <- as.matrix(model$posterior$a$coeffs)
  k <- model$model$k
  # Regressors: the lags of s and n, the error correction term, the constant
  coef <- function(eq, reg) draws[, (reg - 1) * k + eq]

  alpha <- cbind(coef(1, 3), coef(2, 3))
  gamma <- list(coef(1, 1), coef(1, 2), coef(2, 1), coef(2, 2))

  z <- rep(-1, nrow(draws))
  ds <- dn <- rep(0, nrow(draws))
  services <- other <- remaining <- matrix(NA_real_, nrow(draws), horizon)
  for (h in 1:horizon) {
    ds_new <- alpha[, 1] * z + gamma[[1]] * ds + gamma[[2]] * dn
    dn_new <- alpha[, 2] * z + gamma[[3]] * ds + gamma[[4]] * dn
    ds <- ds_new
    dn <- dn_new
    z <- z + ds - dn
    services[, h] <- 100 * ds
    other[, h] <- -100 * dn
    remaining[, h] <- -100 * z
  }

  data.frame(h = 0:horizon,
             remaining = c(100, apply(remaining, 2, median)),
             lower = c(100, apply(remaining, 2, quantile, probs = 0.16)),
             upper = c(100, apply(remaining, 2, quantile, probs = 0.84)),
             services = c(0, apply(t(apply(services, 1, cumsum)), 2, median)),
             other = c(0, apply(t(apply(other, 1, cumsum)), 2, median)))
}

Half of the gap is closed between quarters 4 and 5, which the paper reports as about five quarters. Here, unlike in the benchmark, both components close the gap, and goods inflation does more of it: at quarter 5 services contribute 17 and goods 41 percentage points, against roughly 15 and 38 in the paper. The goods loading is positive, and its 95% credible interval excludes zero: when services are cheap relative to goods, goods inflation falls. The faster adjustment reflects the energy and food prices in the goods index, which move much more than NEIG prices and return quickly after a shock.

Deviations from the paper

  • Data before 2000. The current vintage of prc_hicp_minr starts in December 1999 for the euro area, so the first four years are back-cast from the ECB’s ECOICOP version 1 indices, and the NEIG index for 1997 to 2000 from its annual rates.
  • Data vintage. The data were downloaded on 12 October 2026 rather than 10 August, so the most recent quarters may include revisions.
  • Trend sample. The paper does not say over which sample the trend of the first stage is estimated. The whole sample, 1996 Q1 to 2026 Q2, reproduces its results. The 1996 to 2019 trend that the paper’s Chart 2 draws instead gives a half-life of 26 to 27 quarters for NEIG and 6 to 7 for goods.
